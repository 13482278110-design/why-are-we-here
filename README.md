# Why Are We Here?

My background is mainly in traditional art and design. When I learned painting and other forms of art, I was used to making things with my own hands, step by step. I could see the process of a work being built, and I usually understood how the changes I made would affect the final result.

With the rise of AI, AI programming and vibe coding have become part of the way we create things. When I started trying them myself, I noticed a few problems.

The first one is about design. Sometimes AI can generate something that works, but I do not like the visual result. I still want to add my own design choices and make it feel more like my work. However, if I rely completely on AI and understand nothing about programming, it is difficult for me to know what is causing the result or which part I should change. I can tell AI that I want something different, but I cannot really change the system myself.

I noticed a similar problem during this course. When I did not understand something in class, I could simply copy it into ChatGPT, ask it to explain it, and then follow its instructions. If an error appeared, I could copy the error back and ask for the next step. In this way, I could finish the task, but the whole process sometimes felt like copying and pasting. After finishing, I might still not know what I had actually done or why it worked.

This made me think that if I continue working in this way, I may become more dependent on AI instead of becoming better at using it.

There is another problem that I find more interesting. When I know nothing about programming, AI can easily become the starting point of my thinking. I imagine it like trying to reach a destination when there are many possible roads, but I do not know any of them. AI shows me one road, so naturally I follow it.

The road may be completely correct. It may even be the easiest or most efficient one. But there may be many other roads that I cannot see simply because I do not know enough about the medium.

For me, this is also where programming starts to become interesting. There can be many ways to reach the same goal. Some may be simple, some may be long or complicated, and some may lead to unexpected results. AI can show me useful and reasonable ways forward, but I do not want those to become the only possibilities I know.

I am starting to think that learning programming is not only about learning how to write code. It is also about understanding what is happening behind the result, being able to make my own changes, and discovering possibilities that I would not know existed if I only followed AI.

The Schotter exercise was the first time I directly felt the artistic side of programming. I was surprised when I first saw how a simple grid of squares could gradually change from order to disorder through code. By changing parameters such as ROWS, COLS, and CHAOS, I could see how small changes in rules created different visual results. It made me realize that programming is not only a tool for producing an image—the rules themselves can also be part of the creative process.

Watching Dylan Beattie’s The Art of Code made me think about this in another way. Before watching it, I mostly saw programming as a practical skill. I thought people wrote code mainly to build useful things, such as websites, apps, or software.

What interested me most in the talk was seeing code used in ways that were not always practical. Beattie showed examples such as generative art, demoscene, esoteric programming languages, and live coding. Some of them were experimental, playful, or simply created because someone wanted to see what was possible

After my first experience with AI-assisted programming, I wanted to know whether this feeling of “finishing without understanding” was only my own problem. Rahe and Maalej (2025) studied how programming students use generative AI and found that students use it both to understand concepts and to generate solutions. What interested me was that AI can support learning, but it can also make it easy to move directly to a solution without fully engaging with the code.

Rahe, C., & Maalej, W. (2025). How do programming students use generative AI? *Proceedings of the ACM on Software Engineering, 2*(FSE), 978–1000. https://doi.org/10.1145/3715762
