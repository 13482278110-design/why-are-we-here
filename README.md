# why-are-we-here

Before taking this course, I had never learned programming. My undergraduate background was mainly in traditional art and design. I was used to creating things step by step, either by hand or with design software, and AI was rarely involved in my work. Most of the time, I could imagine how the changes I made would affect the final result.

During these two weeks, I have relied on AI a lot. When I did not understand something in class, I copied it into ChatGPT. When I did not know what to type in the Terminal, I asked AI. If an error appeared, I copied it back and asked what to do next. Most of the time, this worked. I could finish the task, but sometimes I still did not really understand what I had done. This experience made me realize that **finishing something is not the same as understanding it**. Similar behavior has also been observed in programming education. Rahe and Maalej (2025) found that students used generative AI both to understand programming concepts and to generate solutions, while also identifying the risk of students becoming overly dependent on generated solutions instead of understanding the code themselves.

At the same time, as technology develops, AI is making programming much more accessible to ordinary people. Sometimes I even feel that AI can handle most of the programming work, especially for someone like me with very little technical background. Vibe coding allows people to describe what they want in natural language and quickly create small programs, websites, or personal tools (Willison, 2025). For example, if I need a simple dashboard for deadlines and daily tasks, I can clearly describe its functions and interactions and let AI handle most of the coding.

But my view changes when programming becomes part of a design or artistic process. Design is not always easy to describe in words. Sometimes I know that a visual result feels wrong, but I cannot explain exactly why. It may feel too regular, too mechanical, or simply different from what I imagined. If I know nothing about programming, I can only keep changing my prompts: “make it more random,” “less regular,” or “try something different.” I am still depending on AI to interpret my feelings for me.

The `Schotter` exercise gave me a different experience. By changing parameters such as `ROWS`, `COLS`, and `CHAOS`, I could directly see how rules in the code changed the visual result. Instead of only asking AI to generate something different, I started changing the system that produced the image. I did not suddenly become a programmer, but I had more control over what I was making.

This also connects to Dylan Beattie’s The Art of Code. Before watching it, I mainly thought of programming as a practical skill for building useful software. One example that particularly interested me was Conway’s Game of Life. It starts with only a few simple rules, but as these rules repeat, they can create complex and unpredictable patterns (Beattie, 2019). This reminded me of the Schotter exercise: the final visual result does not have to be designed directly. Instead, we can design the rules that generate it. This made me realize that code is not only used to solve problems; it can also be a material for experimentation and creative expression.

This idea feels familiar to me as a design student. In traditional art, understanding paint, color, composition, and other materials gives me more ways to express an idea. I do not learn a material simply to finish my work faster. I learn it because **understanding the medium changes what I am able to create**. I am starting to see programming in the same way.

AI can help me make things faster. I am not learning programming to compete with AI at writing code faster, but to understand what AI creates. Learning and understanding programming can also give me more control over my own work. When a result does not match my intention, I want to be able to change the rules instead of only changing the prompt. It also gives me more freedom to explore possibilities that may be difficult to describe in words and to decide for myself what my work could become.
